What should commercial flat-roof service include?

A commercial flat roof is usually a low-slope roof, and its performance depends on more than the visible membrane. A contractor should assess the roof assembly, seams, flashing, penetrations, drains, scuppers, edge details, and any equipment supports. The inspection should also look for signs of trapped moisture, ponding water, movement, and damage around rooftop units.

Ask for findings in writing, with photos and a clear scope of work. A useful proposal separates immediate repairs from recommended maintenance or larger future work, and explains which roof areas are included. If the roof has several layers or past repairs, ask how those conditions affect the proposed solution.

What does a local professional check during an inspection?

Denver's changing weather can put stress on roof materials and drainage. A professional should check whether water is moving toward drains, whether drains and scuppers are clear, and whether ponding is tied to a blockage, slope, or a larger roof-system issue. They should also look at the condition of flashing and sealant, which can fail around penetrations even when the main roof surface appears sound.

The inspection should account for access, building use, and any equipment or tenant activity that could affect the work. Ask the contractor to distinguish what they observed from what needs further testing. If moisture beneath the surface is possible, ask how they will verify it before recommending a repair or replacement.

Should you repair, restore, or replace the roof?

A repair may make sense when damage is limited and the surrounding roof system remains serviceable. The contractor should identify the source of the problem and explain how the repair will tie into the existing material. A recurring leak can point to an unresolved detail or a broader issue, so ask what evidence supports a repair-only recommendation.

Restoration may be an option when the existing roof is a suitable base and its condition supports the proposed system. Replacement may be needed when damage, moisture, age, or previous work makes a repair or coating unreliable. Ask the contractor to explain the expected service life and limitations of each option without treating a coating as a fix for every roof.

What drives the cost of a commercial flat roof project?

There is no dependable price without an inspection and defined scope. Cost can change with roof size, access, roof height, material type, layers to remove, wet or damaged insulation, drainage work, flashing details, and the amount of rooftop equipment. Disposal, safety planning, occupied-building coordination, and the need to protect inventory or operations can also affect the proposal.

Compare bids line by line rather than choosing by the total alone. Check whether each proposal includes tear-off, insulation, membrane or coating, flashing, drain work, cleanup, permits if required, and warranty details. Ask what conditions could lead to additional work and how you will approve any change before it proceeds.

How do weather, access, and building use affect timing?

The schedule depends on the roof's condition, project size, material availability, crew availability, and weather. Rain, snow, wind, or temperatures outside a product's application requirements can delay some work. A contractor should explain how the roof will be protected if work is paused and how a forecast change affects the plan.

Commercial work may also need to be staged around tenants, deliveries, business hours, rooftop equipment, or restricted access. Ask for an expected start window, work sequence, daily cleanup plan, and a way to report urgent concerns during the project. Confirm whether the building needs to close or limit access to any area.

What mistakes should you avoid before hiring?

  • Choosing a bid based only on its total, without comparing materials, prep, exclusions, and warranty terms.
  • Accepting a patch recommendation without asking the contractor to identify the source of the leak and inspect nearby details.
  • Assuming a coating can solve wet insulation, failed drainage, or structural concerns.
  • Leaving access, tenant communication, cleanup, or protection of equipment out of the written scope.
  • Treating a verbal promise as part of the job. Ask for the agreed scope and any changes in writing.

Call a qualified roofing professional promptly if water is entering the building, the roof surface is visibly damaged, or drains are backing up. If there is an immediate safety concern, keep people away from the affected area and follow your building's emergency procedures while arranging an inspection.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • Have you worked on this type of commercial roof assembly and a building of similar use?
  • What did you find during the inspection, and can you show me photos of the affected areas?
  • Why do you recommend this repair, restoration, or replacement option over the alternatives?
  • What materials, preparation, flashing, drainage work, and cleanup are included in the written scope?
  • What is excluded, and how will you price and get approval for unexpected work?
  • How will you protect the building and coordinate with tenants, staff, or rooftop equipment?
  • What warranties apply to the materials and workmanship, and what conditions could limit them?
  • Who will supervise the project, and how will we handle schedule updates or urgent issues?
